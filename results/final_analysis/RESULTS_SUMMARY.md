## Executive summary (plain English)

- **Fragmented prose stays highly reconstructable for a strong LLM attacker.** On the 46 untouched headline pools, one-shot Sonnet reached F1_J 0.723 [0.702, 0.743] against an analytic baseline of 0.149 (normalized advantage 0.673 [0.649, 0.698]). It recovered most membership (linkage component F1_L 0.882) and most order (q_micro 0.792). Yet it reconstructed only 1 of 322 documents perfectly: the output is a strong partial reconstruction, not verbatim recovery.
- **Every prose condition leaks.** Across the nine grid cells Sonnet's F1_J ran from 0.498 (subject-verb / clause) to 0.801 (random / sentence), against baselines of 0.128–0.132. The smallest advantage anywhere is 0.422. No tested strategy or granularity brings prose near the baseline.
- **Strategy and granularity still matter.** Subject-verb cutting was the most resistant at both of its granularities; at the same granularity it lowered F1_J by 0.18–0.26 relative to every other strategy (paired CIs exclude 0). Within most strategies, finer cuts lowered F1_J modestly.
- **Given membership, ordering is largely recoverable.** In game O Sonnet's Kendall τ was 0.769 on headline documents and 0.724–0.943 across grid cells. The classical Jaccard floors sat at chance (τ -0.061 to 0.024).
- **Identifiers behave completely differently.** On the 20 identifier pools Sonnet scored F1_J 0.083 [0.068, 0.099]: below the analytic baseline (0.10) and far below the schema-order baseline (0.20). It recognised the record template perfectly (every one of its 140 groups held exactly one name, SSN, account and address), but paired fields across records at a rate of 0.132 [0.113, 0.154], consistent with the 1/7 of template-respecting guessing. The frozen near-baseline rule's 90% CI on the normalized schema advantage is [-0.162, -0.129], entirely below −0.10: **not near the baseline; below it.**
- **The statistical attackers are floors.** Memon-inspired scored F1_J 0.168 on the headline (advantage 0.023) and 0.126–0.142 on the grid; its ordering is at chance (q ≈ 0.5). Sonnet beat it on every one of the 215 paired grid pools and all 46 headline pools, by 0.367–0.670 per cell.
- **The post-hoc exploratory embedding attacker** (all-mpnet-base-v2 grouping + the Memon-inspired ordering; F1_L is its primary comparison). Headline F1_L 0.364 [0.335, 0.397], against Memon-inspired 0.339 and Sonnet 0.882. Paired: embedding − Memon-inspired 0.024 [0.005, 0.044] (higher); Sonnet − embedding 0.518 [0.487, 0.547] (higher). On the grid, embedding − Memon-inspired F1_L ranged -0.016 to 0.030 across cells (higher in 2 of 9), and Sonnet − embedding 0.395 to 0.614. Secondary: headline F1_J 0.182, q_micro 0.500. Identifiers: F1_J 0.054, F1_L 0.109. The embedding attacker is POST-HOC EXPLORATORY (RUN_DECISIONS D14): it was motivated by the observed gap between Memon-inspired and one-shot Sonnet, then fully designed and frozen before its own evaluation, with no final-data tuning and no model selection from its scores; this is its first complete result, kept as is. F1_L is its primary comparison; F1_J and q are secondary components. It is not a prespecified or confirmatory attacker.
- **Resource limits rarely bound the one-shot attacker.** One of 652 one-shot units hit the 128k output budget: random__token-window pool_002 (322 fragments). Output tokens grow with fragment count (ρ 0.739), but the largest completed pool had 537 fragments, so size alone does not explain the non-completion.

## Direct answers, without a defence bias

- **Where fragmentation helped:** identifiers, fully (attackers fell below trivial baselines). In prose, only relatively: subject-verb cutting cut F1_J to 0.498, versus 0.739 for ordinary sentence splitting.
- **Where it did not:** every prose condition. The weakest defence (random / sentence) left F1_J at 0.801; even the strongest left a normalized advantage of 0.422.
- **Where reconstruction stayed strong:** membership (F1_L 0.646–0.879 across cells) and ordering given membership (τ 0.724–0.943). Exact whole-document recovery was rare everywhere.
- **Linkage or ordering?** Both, strategy-specifically (§3): subject-verb and random token windows act mainly on linkage; semantic-style boundaries act mainly on ordering.
- **Granularity:** mattered modestly within three of four strategies; confounded with fragment count and size.
- **Boundary strategy:** mattered more than granularity in this grid (subject-verb vs the rest, 0.18–0.26 F1_J).
- **Fragment count, length, size:** associated (count and size strongly, length mainly through ordering); the design supports no causal claim.
- **Identifiers vs prose:** qualitatively different: no recoverable membership signal beyond the schema, versus strong signal in all prose.
- **Statistical attackers:** floors. Memon-inspired was at most 0.023 above the analytic baseline (headline advantage), and the Jaccard orderers were at chance in game O.
- **Did modern sentence embeddings close the gap?** (post-hoc exploratory; F1_L primary) Headline F1_L: embedding 0.364 vs Memon-inspired 0.339 (higher) vs Sonnet 0.882 (Sonnet − embedding 0.518 [0.487, 0.547]).
- **How much stronger Sonnet is:** +0.554 F1_J over Memon-inspired on the headline, +0.367 to +0.670 per grid cell, and +0.733 to +0.996 Kendall τ over the Jaccard floors in game O (all paired).
- **Where resource limits mattered:** one pool (random__token-window pool_002). Otherwise resources shaped cost, not completion: the most fragmented cells cost the most per pool (subject-verb / clause $0.66 vs semantic / sentence $0.20).

## 3. Linkage vs ordering (§14, diagnostic)

F1_J = F1_L × q holds exactly per pool (max residual 2.2e-16). Reading the paired decompositions above:

- **Subject-verb cutting mainly breaks linkage.** Semantic − subject-verb at sentence granularity: ΔF1_L 0.190 [0.166, 0.216] but Δq only 0.036 [0.022, 0.050].
- **The semantic rule (vs random boundaries) mainly makes ordering harder, not linkage.** Random − semantic at sentence granularity: ΔF1_L -0.017 [-0.047, 0.014] (indistinguishable), Δq 0.090 [0.069, 0.111]. The same pattern holds at clause granularity.
- **Random token windows break linkage more than coherence-sever windows** (ΔF1_L -0.109 [-0.134, -0.083]) while being slightly easier to order (Δq 0.033 [0.012, 0.054]).
- **Game O agrees that ordering alone is not the defence.** With membership given, every prose cell is reordered well above chance; the most resistant cells there are again subject-verb (τ ≈ 0.72–0.73).
- So resistance comes from **both** components, with the larger and more strategy-specific share coming from linkage (subject-verb, random token-window) and a consistent ordering penalty from semantic-style boundaries.

## 6. Relationships (§8–13, association only)

- **Document length.** Headline J: ρ(mean document words, F1_J) = -0.129 [-0.404, 0.162], i.e. no clear association with overall reconstruction, but ρ with q = -0.451 [-0.695, -0.155]: longer documents were harder to order. Game O: ρ(words, τ) = -0.351 [-0.503, -0.173] (headline) and -0.252 [-0.449, -0.026] (grid, document-clustered). Headline vs grid reference: F1_J difference -0.016 [-0.054, 0.021] despite 67 more fragments per pool (unpaired; `table_H_headline_vs_grid`).
- **Fragment count.** Pooled over the grid, ρ(fragments per pool, F1_J) = -0.695 [-0.745, -0.641]; within cells the association is negative in 9 of 9 cells, with the CI excluding 0 in 5. On the headline it is -0.193 [-0.451, 0.088]. Game O: ρ(fragments, τ) = -0.290 [-0.456, -0.129]. Fragment count is confounded with cell (strategy × granularity) and, within a cell, with document length; this is an association, not a causal effect of fragment count.
- **Fragment size.** Pooled grid ρ(mean fragment words, F1_J) = 0.654 [0.590, 0.716] (smaller fragments, lower reconstruction); in game O ρ(mean fragment words, τ) = 0.108 [-0.019, 0.235]: fragment size was strongly associated with joint reconstruction and linkage, and only weakly with ordering once membership is given.
- **Granularity** (paired within strategy, §2 above): finer is lower for random (sentence → clause → token-window), semantic and subject-verb; coherence-sever clause vs token-window is not distinguishable in F1_J. Granularity changes fragment count and size together, so the boundary rule itself is not isolated as the cause (§8).

## 9. The plan's fifteen questions (§30)

1. **What was easiest to reconstruct?** random / sentence in game J (F1_J 0.801); in game O, random / sentence and coherence-sever / token-window (τ ≈ 0.94).
2. **What was hardest?** Identifiers (F1_J 0.083, below trivial baselines). Among prose, subject-verb / clause (F1_J 0.498).
3. **Which strategy performed best and worst (as a defence)?** Best: subject-verb, at both granularities. Worst: random boundaries at sentence granularity.
4. **Did granularity matter?** Modestly: finer cuts lowered F1_J within random, semantic and subject-verb (paired CIs exclude 0); not distinguishable for coherence-sever. Confounded with fragment count and size.
5. **Did document length matter?** Not clearly for overall J; longer documents were associated with lower ordering accuracy (headline q; game O τ).
6. **Did fragment count matter?** Associated with lower reconstruction pooled and within most cells; confounded, not causal.
7. **Did fragment size matter?** Smaller fragments were associated with lower linkage and F1_J; weakly with order.
8. **Linkage or ordering failure?** Both; the larger strategy-specific effects are on linkage (subject-verb, random token-window); semantic-style boundaries add an ordering penalty.
9. **Did identifiers behave differently from prose?** Yes, qualitatively: linkage at template chance and F1_J below trivial baselines, versus strong reconstruction everywhere in prose.
10. **Which attacker was strongest?** One-shot Sonnet, by the explicit rule 'highest mean headline F1_J among prespecified attackers completing at least 90% of pools', and on every grid cell. The post-hoc exploratory embedding attacker is reported alongside but is outside that rule. Hierarchical pending.
11. **Where did resource limits bite?** Once: random__token-window pool_002 exhausted 128k. Nowhere else.
12. **What can we claim?** See CLAIM_EVIDENCE.md: the prose-leakage, strategy, attacker and narrow identifier claims.
13. **What can we NOT claim?** That fragmentation protects prose; any causal effect of fragment count, size or length; anything about the hierarchical attacker; equivalence from nonsignificance; generality beyond these corpora, this model and these attackers; that the statistical floors are pre-LLM state of the art.
14. **What was surprising?** Random sentence-level boundaries were the easiest to invert; coherence-sever token windows were the easiest to order given membership yet kept high linkage; full-length headline documents were not harder than the shorter grid reference; the one non-completion was not the largest pool; Sonnet reconstructs the identifier template perfectly while linking at chance.
15. **Main paper vs appendix?** Main: Tables B, C, E and a condensed D; Figures 1, 2, 3, 5 and 4. Appendix: relationships (Figures 6–8, Table R), resources (Figure 10, Table G), mechanics, the full paired tables, the error taxonomy, the SHA-256 reproducibility report and the screening history.
